Can physics-guided machine learning make wearable electrochemical biosensor calibration more reliable?

A lightweight neural network adds a monotonicity constraint to electrochemical calibration, reducing error on a public wearable sweat-biosensor dataset while preserving physically plausible response trends.

Editorial note by EchemAI

In brief

ResultThe physics-guided model reported RMSE 0.9077 versus 1.8547 for linear regression and R2 = 0.7940 versus 0.1400, corresponding to about a 49% reduction in RMSE.
Why it mattersThe study shows a practical use of domain knowledge as a training constraint: the AI model is asked to respect a physically meaningful response trend instead of learning calibration from data alone.
Physics-informed machine learning for robust calibration and physiological validation of wearable electrochemical sweat biosensors for metabolite monitoring
Umapathi Krishnamoorthy et al. · Scientific Reports · Published 28 September 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s41598-026-69813-8

The electrochemical problem

Wearable electrochemical biosensors operate under variable physiological and environmental conditions. A calibration obtained from one set of measurements can drift as sensor response, sweat composition, and subject-specific factors change.

Purely linear calibration is attractive because it is simple and interpretable, but it can miss nonlinear response structure. A data-driven model can capture that nonlinearity, although unconstrained fitting may also learn relationships that are inconsistent with the expected electrochemical response.

What the researchers did

The authors analyzed a public dataset of voltammetric wearable-sweat measurements. Three electrochemical features, peak current, peak potential, and mean current, were used to train a small neural network.

The loss function combined prediction error with a soft monotonicity penalty, encouraging concentration predictions to follow the expected direction of sensor response. The model was compared with ordinary linear regression using RMSE, R2, leave-one-out cross-validation, noise tests, residual analysis, and Bland-Altman analysis.

What they found

The physics-guided model achieved an RMSE of 0.9077 and R2 of 0.7940, compared with RMSE 1.8547 and R2 0.1400 for the linear model. The authors describe this as about a 49% reduction in prediction error.

The study also examined sweat-serum relationships, reporting moderate R2 values of about 0.48 for branched-chain amino acids and 0.57 for leucine, and used subject-wise time series to examine individual metabolite trends.

Why this matters

This is a clear example of domain knowledge entering AI at the level of the loss function. The sensor hardware is unchanged; the improvement comes from telling the learning algorithm that some response relationships should be physically ordered.

For electrochemical sensing, this approach is attractive because many calibration problems have partial physical knowledge even when a full mechanistic model is impractical.

One important caution

The study uses a previously published dataset rather than newly acquired measurements from a prospectively operated wearable device. The model is trained offline and uses only three extracted electrochemical features.

The paper explicitly notes that real-time operation, direct drift compensation, broader population validation, and deployment on new sensor hardware remain future work. The result is therefore a calibration-method demonstration, not a clinically validated wearable system.
