Can machine learning guide a four-electron pathway in lithium–oxygen batteries?

A literature-trained model, electronic-structure calculations, and experiments connect catalyst selection with oxygen-reduction chemistry and cell cycling.

Editorial note by EchemAI

In brief

QuestionCan data help identify a promising dual-metal active site for a lithium–oxygen electrode?
ResultWith 5 vol% water, the catalyst delivered about 60,000 mAh g−1 in a full-discharge test and 2,075 cycles in a separate capacity-limited test.
Why it mattersThe study connects literature-based screening to calculations, synthesis, and electrochemical testing, while illustrating why performance must be read with its test conditions and mass basis.
Machine learning-driven regulation of the four-electron LiOH pathway in lithium–oxygen batteries with prolonged cycling
Tiansheng Bai, Yiming Hu, Wei Zhai, Xiongwei Zhong et al. · Nature Communications 17, 11801 · Published 18 November 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s41467-026-79335-6

The electrochemical problem

The conventional two-electron oxygen-reduction pathway in lithium–oxygen batteries forms insulating lithium peroxide (Li₂O₂), which can hinder reactions and charging at the oxygen electrode. The authors sought a catalyst that could promote a proton-coupled, four-electron route to lithium hydroxide (LiOH). The large number of possible metal combinations makes choosing a catalyst for synthesis a substantial challenge.

What the researchers did

The team assembled thousands of literature-derived data points with nearly 50 atomic, electronic, and physical descriptors and compared eight regression models for targets including overpotential. XGBoost achieved a mean R² of 0.8815 ± 0.0346 across five validation folds grouped by source publication. Grouping reduces the risk that records from the same paper appear in both training and testing, but is not an independent test under entirely new laboratory conditions.

SHAP analysis highlighted features including metal d-electron count and non-metal electronegativity. The model was used to predict combinations of 28 candidate elements, identifying Co–Ni as promising. The researchers then used density functional theory (DFT) to examine electronic structures and reaction intermediates, synthesized Ni–Co dual-atom catalysts on nitrogen-doped reduced graphene oxide, and prepared single-atom controls. Atomic-resolution microscopy, X-ray absorption analysis, diffraction, and electrochemical measurements were used to examine structure and performance.

What they found

In a full-discharge test with 5 vol% water at 200 mA g−1, the reported capacity was about 60,000 mAh g−1 based on catalyst mass. The authors also express this as approximately 3,600 mAh g−1 based on the total gas-diffusion-layer electrode mass and 35 mAh cm−2 based on geometric area. This water-containing full-discharge test used a LiFePO₄ negative electrode. Diffraction results support a shift in the discharged product toward crystalline LiOH as water content increases.

In a separate cycling test with 5 vol% water, a catalyst-mass-normalized current of 500 mA g−1, and a capacity limit of 500 mAh g−1, the Ni–Co catalyst reached 2,075 cycles versus 790 for the Co single-atom control. It is therefore incorrect to interpret the result as 2,075 cycles each delivering 60,000 mAh g−1. Gas analysis gave a discharge electron-to-oxygen ratio of 3.66 for Ni–Co, which the authors interpret as a mixture of two- and four-electron processes.

The authors also tested the catalyst architecture as a protective layer on lithium metal and reported suppression of dendritic growth. The separate cycling test with this protected lithium electrode used an electrolyte without added water.

Why this matters

The contribution is not the first demonstration of LiOH-based four-electron chemistry. Rather, the study links a literature-based catalyst selection step with DFT, synthesis, and battery experiments. Machine learning narrows the experimental search; it does not by itself establish the electrochemical mechanism, which requires separate evidence from diffraction, spectroscopy, and other measurements.

Strengths and limitations

Source-grouped cross-validation, single-atom controls, structural characterization, and cell tests strengthen the study. The authors report that the data and a code snapshot are available. The literature data nonetheless span different compositions and measurement conditions, and missing or nonnumeric descriptors were set to zero. The effects of that choice and generalization to genuinely new catalysts or laboratories warrant further testing. SHAP importance is not proof of causation.

Added water, capacity limitation, catalyst-mass normalization, and the choice of negative electrode distinguish these tests from a practical full cell. Long-term lithium-metal stability in water-containing electrolytes, whole-cell energy, and reproducibility across independent experiments need further assessment. The results alone do not establish commercial readiness or generalization to other electrolytes. This section and the discussion of significance are EchemAI editorial interpretation.
