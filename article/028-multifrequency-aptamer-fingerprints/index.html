Can multifrequency voltammetry distinguish targets that bind the same aptamer?

Frequency-dependent electrochemical responses provide a fingerprint that a random forest uses to identify and quantify two closely related antibiotics.

Editorial note by EchemAI · Literature window: 16–22 October 2026

In brief

QuestionCan interrogation at multiple frequencies recover selectivity from a cross-reactive aptamer sensor?
ResultThe authors report 80% classification accuracy and regression R² values of 0.91 for tobramycin and 0.93 for kanamycin.
Why it mattersSelectivity becomes a joint property of the sensing chemistry, the electrochemical waveform and the analysis model.
Multifrequency Electrochemical, Aptamer-Based Sensor Fingerprints Enable Machine Learning Identification and Quantification of Cross-Reactive Targets
Swati Singh and Ryan J. White · ACS Measurement Science Au · Published online 19 October 2026
DOI: 10.1021/acsmeasuresciau.6c00285

The electrochemical problem

An aptamer can bind several chemically related molecules. If their conventional voltammetric responses have similar magnitudes, a single current measurement cannot reliably identify which species is present. The kinetic response of a binding-dependent electrochemical signal may provide another measurement dimension.

What the researchers did

Singh and White used an aminoglycoside-responsive electrochemical aptamer sensor and interrogated it by continuous square-wave voltammetry. Instead of reducing the response to one signal gain, they used its variation with interrogation frequency. A random forest was trained to identify the target and estimate its concentration.

What they found

Tobramycin and kanamycin gave similar responses in conventional measurements but could be differentiated using the multifrequency signatures. Across multiple electrodes, the abstract reports classification accuracy of 0.80 and concentration-regression R² of 0.91 and 0.93 for the two antibiotics, respectively.

Why this matters

The model has access to a richer signal because the experiment deliberately probes a frequency dimension. The design principle is to produce informative electrochemical differences before asking a classifier to resolve them. It is particularly relevant to sensors with limited molecular specificity.

One important caution

An accuracy of 80% still leaves classification errors. The abstract does not establish the performance in simultaneous mixtures, unfamiliar electrode batches or complex clinical matrices. Detailed validation splits and uncertainty estimates could not be checked from the accessible record; the reported R² values should be interpreted within the authors’ evaluation.
