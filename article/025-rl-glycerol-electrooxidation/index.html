Can reinforcement learning discover a better way to operate an electrochemical reaction?

A recurrent reinforcement-learning controller changes the applied potential in response to measured current and learns how to sustain glycerol oxidation on platinum.

Editorial note by EchemAI · Literature window: 2–8 October 2026

In brief

QuestionCan adaptive potential control recover activity as the platinum surface changes during glycerol oxidation?
ResultThe learned protocol increased the mean charge-transfer rate by approximately 30% relative to the authors’ engineered three-step protocol.
Why it mattersAI becomes part of the electrochemical control loop, rather than a tool used only to screen catalyst compositions.
AI-driven electrocatalysis: reinforcement learning-based control and theoretical modeling of the glycerol oxidation reaction
Vladislav A. Mints, Maria-Magdalena Titirici and Ifan E. L. Stephens · Energy & Environmental Science · Published online 5 October 2026
DOI: 10.1039/d6ee02239a

The electrochemical problem

Glycerol oxidation can deactivate platinum through the accumulation of adsorbed species. Pulsing the potential can restore activity, but a fixed sequence must trade productive oxidation time against regeneration time. An adaptive controller can use the changing current as feedback.

What the researchers did

The team developed a recurrent Q-learning controller and connected it to a potentiostat operating a Pt rotating disk in alkaline glycerol solution. An initial kinetic simulation and subsequent experimentally trained recurrent models supported iterative controller development. The system selected potentials from measured current and voltage; each action was nominally held for 100 ms.

What they found

The resulting policy combined frequent relaxation at 0.3 V versus RHE with less frequent excursions to 1.0 V before returning to the relaxation potential. Its oxidation step was applied at 0.85 V in the experimental setup to compensate for solution resistance. The reported average gain in charge-transfer rate was about 30% against the engineered comparator. A separate neural model incorporated a trainable Butler–Volmer layer to describe evolving surface activity.

Why this matters

The important distinction is between designing a catalyst and designing its operation. A feedback policy can exploit a time-dependent electrode state even when that state is only partly observable. The learned sequence offers a specific electrochemical hypothesis about short and long regeneration events that can be tested with synchronized spectroscopy.

One important caution

The reward was charge-transfer rate, not the yield or Faradaic efficiency of an identified glycerol oxidation product. The proposed identities and roles of poisoning species remain mechanistic hypotheses; the authors explicitly discuss the need for operando verification. The reported improvement is specific to the tested cell and comparison protocol.
