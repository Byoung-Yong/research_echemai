Can a neural network choose an equivalent-circuit class from an impedance spectrum?

CNNs classify Nyquist plots before conventional parameter fitting, with transfer learning and an experimental pyrrole electropolymerization demonstration.

QuestionCan a neural network choose an equivalent-circuit class from an impedance spectrum?
ResultThe published article reports >80% classification accuracy for spectra generated with random parameters.
Why it mattersAI is inserted at the model-selection step, one of the most analyst-dependent parts of equivalent-circuit EIS analysis.
Determination of equivalent circuits for electrochemical impedance spectra using convolutional neural networks
Long Duong Ha et al. · npj Computational Materials · Published 11 July 2026
DOI: 10.1038/s41524-026-02225-4

The electrochemical problem

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy does not interpret itself. A measured spectrum must be connected to a model before fitted parameters can be given physical meaning. In equivalent-circuit analysis, this means choosing a circuit whose elements represent the processes thought to contribute to the frequency-dependent response.

That model choice is often guided by experience and by the visible shape of the Nyquist plot. The paper asks whether part of this analyst-dependent step can be standardized with machine learning.

What the researchers did

The authors treat equivalent-circuit selection as a supervised image-classification problem. Convolutional neural networks extract characteristic features from Nyquist plots and assign spectra to the equivalent-circuit classes represented during training.

Transfer learning is incorporated so pretrained kernels can be reused when adapting the workflow to new data, and the method is also demonstrated on an experimental pyrrole electropolymerization system.

What they found

The published article reports greater than 80% classification accuracy for electrochemical impedance spectra generated with random circuit parameters. The transfer-learning strategy is intended to make the learned feature extraction more reusable when moving to new datasets.

The experimental application shows how the classifier can be placed in a real EIS workflow rather than remaining only a synthetic-spectrum benchmark.

Why this matters

The important change is not faster nonlinear least-squares fitting. AI enters one step earlier, when the analyst chooses which model class should be fitted in the first place.

That could be useful for high-throughput EIS and automated experiments, where manual inspection of every spectrum becomes a bottleneck.

One important caution

A correct classification within a set of candidate circuits is not the same as proving that the corresponding physical mechanism is unique. Different mechanisms can generate similar impedance responses over a finite frequency range, and equivalent circuits themselves can be non-unique.

The reported accuracy therefore supports discrimination among the circuit classes represented in the classification problem. It does not by itself establish mechanistic uniqueness, parameter identifiability, or exclusion of alternative physical models.
