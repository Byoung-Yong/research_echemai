Can a machine-learning surrogate make multiobjective PEM-electrolyzer design fast enough to optimize?

Surrogate models learn from a physical PEMWE simulation and optimize two-dimensional catalyst-loading distributions for current and thermal uniformity.

Machine Learning-Optimized Design of Two-Dimensional MEA Parameters for Enhanced PEM Electrolyzer Performance
Zhenyu Liu et al. · Industrial & Engineering Chemistry Research · Published 4 June 2026
DOI: 10.1021/acs.iecr.6c00681

The electrochemical problem

A PEM water electrolyzer is spatially nonuniform. Catalyst loading, current density, reactant transport, and temperature can vary across the membrane-electrode assembly, so maximizing one output alone can create undesirable local conditions.

High-fidelity multiphysics simulations can evaluate these tradeoffs but are too expensive to run inside a large multiobjective optimization loop.

What the researchers did

The authors generated training data from a PEMWE physical model and evaluated support-vector regression, decision-tree regression, and feed-forward neural networks as surrogate models. The spatial gradient of catalyst loading was parametrized as part of the input.

The surrogate was then used in a weighted multiobjective optimization balancing current density, current-density uniformity, and temperature uniformity.

What they found

Compared with the benchmark model, the optimized MEA parameters increased modeled current density from 1.28 to 1.46 A cm−2, corresponding to a 14.5% improvement, while maintaining acceptable uniformity metrics.

The point is computational efficiency: once trained, the surrogate can evaluate many candidate designs much faster than repeatedly solving the full physical model.

Why this matters

This is a useful hybrid pattern. The physics model provides structure and generates physically constrained training data; machine learning provides speed; optimization explores design tradeoffs.

Such surrogate workflows are especially attractive in electrochemical engineering because device design often involves many geometric and material variables coupled through nonlinear transport equations.

One important caution

The reported improvement is a simulation result, not yet an experimental gain measured in a fabricated MEA. The surrogate can only be as reliable as the physical model and parameter ranges used to train it.

Experimental manufacturing constraints and degradation may also change which catalyst-loading gradients are practical or optimal.
